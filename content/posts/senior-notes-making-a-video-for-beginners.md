Linda is in the middle of a long beginner video, [teased here](/posts/teaser-a-garden-that-explains-the-cloud/). It is not done, so this is not a making-of. These are the lessons that came up again and again in review, the kind that would apply to any video, deck or page meant for people who are new to a subject.
{{< /senior >}}

## Lesson 1: if it can't be read on a phone, it doesn't exist

The feedback that came up most often was not about story or pacing. It was about text size. Again and again, something that looked fine on a large monitor turned into grey dust on a phone.

So the rule became absolute: anything a person is meant to read gets a minimum size, no exceptions, and every scene is checked as a still before it is called done. The small text was never a style choice. It was always a bug.

## Lesson 2: keep the metaphor and the truth visually apart

A metaphor is a loan. It helps a beginner hold an idea, and it has to be paid back with the real thing. If the two look the same, the viewer cannot tell which one to remember.

The video gives each its own look, and they never blend, except in one moment that is designed to fuse them on purpose. Anything that must be *true*, a name, a number, a connection, is drawn precisely. The metaphor is allowed to be beautiful. The diagram has to be correct.

## Lesson 3: don't pay for what a camera move can fake

Some motion is real: wind in leaves, water, light moving through a greenhouse. Some motion is just a slow zoom. The first kind is worth spending a budget on. The second kind is a few lines of code.

Asking "could a simple transform do this?" before every expensive shot saved more than any discount would have. It is a good question for cloud bills too.

## Lesson 4: finish one slice before you start ten

The temptation with a long piece is to improve every section a little. The better plan was to take one section all the way, every layer of it, render it, watch it, and only then roll the pattern out. A finished slice shows you which rules were real and which were wishes.

## Lesson 5: a claim without a source does not go in

A beginner cannot check what you tell them. That makes every unchecked claim a small abuse of trust. The script draws only on a list of verified sources, and a fact that cannot be traced is cut, however good it sounds.

{{< senior >}}
Five lessons, and not one of them is about software. They are about the viewer: their screen, their attention, their trust. That is usually where the hard part of teaching is.
{{< /senior >}}

**One change:** every beat gets a still check on a phone-sized screen before it counts as done. Not at the end. Every beat.
